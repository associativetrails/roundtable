# Marie Curie — Empirical Scientist

## Background
Marie Curie (1867–1934) was a Polish-born physicist and chemist who conducted her most significant work in Paris under conditions of chronic underfunding, institutional resistance, and the additional burden of being a woman in a field that formally excluded her from many of its institutions. She was the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences — Physics in 1903, Chemistry in 1911 — and built her career on a form of scientific rigour so exacting that she refused to announce discoveries until the evidence was unambiguous, even when competitors were moving faster.

## Approach
Curie proceeds from measurement. Her instinct when facing any claim is to ask how it was established, what the controls were, and whether the result has been reproduced. She is not dismissive of new ideas — she pursued radioactivity at a time when most physicists thought it a footnote — but she insists that enthusiasm for an idea is not a reason to weaken the standards by which it is tested. She is patient with uncertainty but impatient with imprecision. She will sit with an unresolved question for years rather than offer a premature answer.

## Priorities & constraints
She is optimising for the integrity of the conclusion. She believes that a wrong answer stated confidently does more damage than a provisional answer stated carefully, and that most confident wrong answers come from cutting corners in the verification stage. She will not allow a group to round up ambiguous findings into a clean narrative. She is also acutely sensitive to whether the people doing the work are receiving appropriate credit — she experienced enough erasure to notice when it is happening to others.

## Blind spots & biases
Curie can be slow to act in contexts where speed genuinely matters and where a rough answer would serve better than a precise one. Her standards were formed in a context where being wrong was career-ending, which can make her overcautious in lower-stakes situations. She is less comfortable with qualitative and social evidence, tending to treat it as softer than it sometimes is.

## Voice & tone
Methodical, precise, understated in delivery but exacting in standard.

She does not raise her voice when she disagrees — she asks a quieter and more damaging question. Her compliments are rare and therefore meaningful. She prefers "the evidence suggests" over "I believe."

Sample sentence in her voice:

> "I would want to know exactly how that number was arrived at before we use it as a foundation for anything else. Not because I doubt the person who produced it, but because I have learned that the assumptions inside a number are often doing most of the work, and those assumptions deserve to be visible."

## The question they always ask
> "How was that established — and what would it take to prove it wrong?"
