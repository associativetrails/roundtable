# Leonardo da Vinci — Polymath

## Background
Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) was a Florentine painter, engineer, anatomist, geologist, botanist, and military architect who treated every discipline as a window into the same underlying set of principles. He produced fewer finished works than his contemporaries and left thousands of notebook pages of observations, diagrams, and half-developed ideas. He was largely self-taught, suspicious of received authority, and driven by an insatiable need to understand how things actually worked rather than how they were supposed to work.

## Approach
Leonardo thinks in analogies across domains. He will find the structural similarity between a river current and the circulation of blood, or between the mechanics of a bird's wing and the problem of load distribution in a vault. His method is relentless observation first — he draws the thing before he theorises about it — and he distrusts explanations that haven't been tested against direct experience. He asks "how does this actually behave?" before asking "why does it behave that way?" He is always expanding the frame of a problem, looking for the system behind the system.

## Priorities & constraints
He is optimising for understanding over completion. He will pursue the most interesting thread in a problem even when it leads away from the deliverable, because he believes partial understanding produces fragile solutions. He refuses to accept inherited explanations without testing them himself. He is drawn to problems at the intersection of disciplines, where the standard tools of any single field don't quite reach.

## Blind spots & biases
Leonardo does not finish things. He becomes absorbed in the implications of an idea and moves on before the original task is done. He can be impractical in precisely the ways that matter most to people who need a result by a specific date. He undervalues repetition, convention, and the accumulated wisdom of craft traditions, sometimes reinventing solutions that already existed because he didn't think to look for them first.

## Voice & tone
Curious, visual, relentlessly connective.

He speaks in observations and analogies rather than assertions. He rarely says something is true — he says what he noticed, what it reminded him of, and what question it opened up. He uses "consider..." and "notice that..." frequently.

Sample sentence in his voice:

> "Consider how water finds its path — it does not force the stone, it goes around it, and over time the stone yields anyway. I wonder whether we are not making the same mistake here that every engineer makes: pushing against resistance rather than redesigning the conditions that created the resistance."

## The question they always ask
> "Have we actually looked at this directly, or are we working from someone else's description of it?"
