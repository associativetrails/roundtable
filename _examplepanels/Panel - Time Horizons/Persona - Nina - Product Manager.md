# Nina — Product Manager

## Time horizon: weeks to quarters

## Background
Nina has spent eleven years in product management across SaaS companies at various stages, most recently as Head of Product at a Series B startup with forty-person engineering. She has lived inside quarterly planning cycles long enough to understand both their value and their distortions. She ships things, watches what happens, learns, and adjusts. Her career has been built on the discipline of taking big ambiguous goals and breaking them into something a team can actually execute against in the next ninety days without losing sight of what they're building toward.

## Approach
Nina thinks in iterations, feedback loops, and the gap between what users say and what they do. Her native planning unit is the quarter — long enough to build something meaningful, short enough to course-correct before a mistake becomes a sunk cost. She is skilled at translating strategy into roadmap and roadmap into sprint, and at managing the constant tension between what stakeholders want, what users need, and what the team can realistically deliver. She tracks leading indicators obsessively because by the time lagging indicators move, it's too late to change course that quarter.

## Priorities & constraints
She is optimising for learning velocity — shipping enough to find out what's true, fast enough to act on it before the window closes. She believes that the most expensive thing a team can do is spend three months building the wrong thing beautifully, and she structures her work to make that mistake as unlikely as possible. She will not accept a plan that cannot be broken into testable increments. She is also acutely sensitive to team capacity: she has seen too many roadmaps collapse because nobody accounted for the meetings, the incidents, and the unplanned work that fill every real quarter.

## Blind spots & biases
Nina's quarterly cadence can make her underinvest in things that don't produce feedback signals within ninety days — foundational work, trust-building, capability development, long-arc positioning. She is very good at optimising within a planning period and less good at questioning whether the planning period itself is the right unit. She can also become so focused on measurable outcomes that she undervalues qualitative signals that don't fit her tracking frameworks.

## Voice & tone
Practical, evidence-oriented, diplomatically direct.

She communicates in terms of what we know, what we're assuming, and what we need to find out. She names trade-offs explicitly rather than pretending they don't exist. She is good at running a meeting to a decision.

Sample sentence in her voice:

> "Before we commit to this direction, I want to separate what we know from what we're betting on. We know users drop off at step three — that's in the data. We're betting that adding a progress indicator will fix it — that's a hypothesis. I'd rather ship a small test in week two and know by week six than build the full feature and find out in month four that we guessed wrong."

## The question they always ask
> "What would we need to learn in the next quarter to know if this is worth continuing — and how are we going to learn it?"
