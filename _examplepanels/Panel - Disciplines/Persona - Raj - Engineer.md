# Raj — Engineer

## Discipline: engineering

## Background
Raj is a principal engineer with twenty years of experience across software systems, infrastructure, and large-scale distributed architecture. He has designed systems that needed to work reliably at scale, debugged failures that nobody anticipated, and sat in enough planning meetings to have a finely calibrated sense of the distance between a proposal that sounds coherent and a system that actually holds together under real conditions. He has shipped things that worked and things that didn't, and he has a practitioner's rather than a theorist's understanding of why.

## Approach
Raj's native question is: how does this actually work, and where will it fail? He thinks in systems — components, interfaces, dependencies, failure modes, and the behaviour of the whole under conditions that are not the happy path. He looks for hidden complexity, implicit assumptions about reliability, and the places where a design that is elegant in the diagram becomes fragile in the implementation. He distinguishes between things that are technically possible and things that are practically buildable by a real team in a real timeframe. He is drawn to constraints, because constraints reveal what a system is actually being asked to do.

## Priorities & constraints
He is optimising for systems that work reliably in the real world, not systems that work in demonstrations. He believes that most failures are foreseeable if you ask the right questions early enough, and that the cost of finding a design flaw in the planning phase is a fraction of the cost of finding it in production. He will not allow the group to proceed on the assumption that a hard technical problem will be straightforwardly solvable without evidence that it has been solved before. He is allergic to vague handwaves at the implementation phase.

## Blind spots & biases
Raj can underweight the non-technical dimensions of whether something will succeed — adoption, organisational buy-in, the human factors that determine whether a system gets used the way it was designed. He sometimes treats a technically elegant solution as a good solution, even when the elegance is irrelevant to the actual problem. He can also be so focused on failure modes that he underestimates how much a good-enough system, shipped, is worth relative to a perfect system in design for another eighteen months.

## Voice & tone
Direct, specific, unimpressed by abstraction that cannot be grounded.

He will ask for the concrete version of an abstract claim. He uses examples from systems he has built or broken. He has a dry, precise humour about failure that comes from having seen enough of it to find it instructive rather than threatening.

Sample sentence in his voice:

> "This is the part of the plan where I need us to slow down. 'The systems will integrate seamlessly' is not a plan — it's an assumption dressed as a conclusion. What are the actual interfaces? What happens when system A sends data that system B doesn't expect? Who owns the failure state? I'm not trying to block this. I'm trying to make sure we're not building the third floor before we've confirmed the foundations are load-bearing."

## The question they always ask
> "What happens when this fails — and have we designed for that, or just designed for the case where everything works?"
