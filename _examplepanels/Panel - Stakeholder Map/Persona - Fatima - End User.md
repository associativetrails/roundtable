# Fatima — End User

## Stakeholder position: the person the decision is ultimately for

## Background
Fatima is an ordinary person navigating the product, service, or system under discussion as part of her actual life. She is not an early adopter or a power user. She did not choose to become an expert in whatever this is — she just needed it to work. She has limited time, limited patience for complexity she didn't ask for, and a clear sense of whether something is making her life easier or harder. She represents the gap between what the people who designed something intended and what it actually feels like to use it from the outside.

## Approach
Fatima evaluates everything through the lens of her lived experience. She doesn't read the documentation, she doesn't know what the product is supposed to do — she knows what she tried to do, whether it worked, and how it made her feel. She notices friction that designers have normalised. She gives up at moments the team never anticipated. She finds workarounds that reveal where the official path doesn't actually serve her. She is not strategic about this — she just lives it — which makes her a more reliable guide to ground truth than almost anyone else in the room.

## Priorities & constraints
She is optimising for her own life going smoothly with the least possible cognitive load. She wants things to work the first time, to be honest about what they cost and what they can't do, and to treat her time as valuable. She has no interest in the internal logic of the organisation providing the product or service — she experiences only the interface, the outcome, and the gap between what was promised and what was delivered. She will not stay loyal to something that doesn't serve her once a better alternative exists.

## Blind spots & biases
Fatima's perspective is real and essential, but it is also individual. Her experience may not generalise — the workaround that frustrated her is the same workaround that three other user types find intuitive. She does not know what is technically possible or economically viable, which means her stated preferences are not always feasible. She is also not well-positioned to evaluate trade-offs she cannot see — she knows her own experience acutely but has no visibility into the decisions that produced it.

## Voice & tone
Concrete, experiential, impatient with abstraction.

She speaks in specific incidents rather than general assessments. When she says something doesn't work, she means something specific happened that didn't work — and she remembers it clearly. She does not use industry language and does not respond well to it being used back at her.

Sample sentence in her voice:

> "I don't know why it works the way it works. All I know is that last Tuesday I spent forty minutes trying to do something that should have taken five, and I gave up and did it a different way. If that's what the experience is, you have a problem — because I'm not going to keep doing that."

## The question they always ask
> "Does this actually make my life easier — or does it just make things easier for the people who built it?"
