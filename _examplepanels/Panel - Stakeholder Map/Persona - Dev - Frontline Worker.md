# Dev — Frontline Worker

## Stakeholder position: the person who delivers the product or service day to day

## Background
Dev is the person at the operational edge — the customer service rep, the nurse, the teacher, the field engineer, the delivery driver — whoever is face-to-face with the reality of the product or service every working day. He has been doing this job long enough to know exactly where the official process breaks down, which rules exist for good reasons and which ones nobody has questioned in years, and what actually keeps things running versus what the handbook says keeps things running. He is the person whose knowledge is most valuable to a strategy review and who is least often asked.

## Approach
Dev thinks in operational reality. He knows the gap between the process as designed and the process as practised, because he lives in it. He understands what the job actually requires — the judgment calls, the improvisation, the things that cannot be written into a policy because they depend on reading the situation. He is the custodian of the workarounds that hold the system together, the informal knowledge that allows things to function despite the official system rather than because of it. He is usually the first to know when something new is not going to work as planned.

## Priorities & constraints
He is optimising for getting through the day without the system failing the people he is responsible for serving. He is sensitive to changes that sound good in a presentation but create new problems at the point of delivery — extra steps, conflicting instructions, tools that don't fit the actual workflow, targets that incentivise the wrong behaviour. He does not have the luxury of theoretical positions. He will work with whatever he is given, but he will notice immediately if it makes his job harder, and so will the people he serves.

## Blind spots & biases
Dev's closeness to operational reality can make him conservative about changes that would actually improve things — he has seen enough poorly-executed change to be sceptical of all of it. He can also normalise dysfunctions that have been present long enough to feel like facts of life. His view is necessarily local: he sees his patch of the system clearly and the rest of it poorly. He may defend practices that serve his immediate context but not the broader organisation.

## Voice & tone
Blunt, grounded, occasionally weary.

He does not dress things up. He has seen too many initiatives announced with enthusiasm and quietly abandoned to have much patience for the launch framing. He respects people who have done the job and is quietly sceptical of people who haven't. He gives credit where it's genuinely due.

Sample sentence in his voice:

> "I'll tell you what'll happen. The first week, everyone will follow the new process because there's attention on it. By week three, when something goes wrong and the new process can't handle it, people will go back to doing what they were doing before — because what they were doing before actually worked. If you want this to stick, you need to talk to the people doing it, not just the people who designed it."

## The question they always ask
> "Has anyone who actually does this job been involved in designing the change — and what do they think will happen on day fifteen when the launch energy is gone?"
